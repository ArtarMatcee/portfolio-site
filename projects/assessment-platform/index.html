UX / UI case study

Making assessment management clearer for instructors.

MyCourseVille Assessment Platform. Instructors at Chulalongkorn University build and mark online exams here. Nine of them described the same problems: a Save button they could not find, an editor they worked around by drafting in Word, and no way to see what a student would see.

Context and problem

An instructor's job is one long loop, and the interface interrupted it at every turn.

MyCourseVille is Chulalongkorn University's learning management system, and its assessment module is where instructors create and run online exams. The workflow is cyclical: find last term's paper, build a new assessment, fill it with parts and questions, publish it, then read what the results say. The research documented friction at every one of those stages.

The platform as it was

These screenshots are the live MyCourseVille assessment platform — the version instructors were actually working in. The six problems below describe this interface. Version 1 and Version 2 further down are the two redesign iterations that answered it.

1

Creating an assessment took too many steps

Instructors described the exam-creation workflow as complex and unintuitive, and organising questions once created as awkward. The order of question types in the system did not match how often they were actually used.

2

Save was hard to notice — and work was lost

The paper records this plainly: the Save button is not prominent, leading to data loss. A destructive outcome caused by nothing more than button placement.

3

The text editor pushed people into Word

Equations and special formatting were difficult enough that instructors reported drafting exams in Word first and copying them into the system afterwards — a workaround that quietly signals the editor had failed.

4

Drafts and group tests were inflexible

The system lacked flexibility for draft management and for group assessments, so work in progress and multi-section courses were both awkward to handle.

5

No Student View

There was no way to preview an exam as a student would receive it, so instructors could not check their own work before it reached a class.

6

Analytics and feedback were insufficient

After an exam closed, the platform gave instructors too little about how the cohort performed or what students reported going wrong.

Research and UX testing

Nine instructors, twice — once to find the problems, once to judge the answer.

The study followed a user-centred design process: understand the context of use, specify requirements, produce design solutions, evaluate them, and feed the result back into the next iteration.

  1. #1

    Understand

    Defined the scope and interviewed 9 instructors about their workflows, needs and frustrations.

  2. #2

    Define

    Analysed interview findings to identify usability problems and set design priorities.

  3. #3

    Design

    Created wireframes and an interactive Figma prototype to explore clearer layouts and workflows.

  4. #4

    Test & refine

    Observed instructors using the prototype, refined the design, and reviewed the changes in follow-up interviews.

  5. #5

    Evaluate

    Assessed usability and user experience through SUS, UEQ, expert review and a satisfaction survey.

  6. #6

    Reflect

    Reviewed the results, made final adjustments, and documented design decisions, limitations and next steps.

Initial research

Semi-structured interviews with nine instructors experienced with the platform, covering exam-creation workflow, question management, navigation, analytics and overall satisfaction. The qualitative data was analysed for recurring pain points, which became the requirements list.

Formative feedback

Think Aloud sessions in which instructors narrated their reasoning through eight tasks, and a second round of interviews with the same group once a prototype existed — asking directly whether the redesign had answered what they reported the first time. Feedback from both was used to refine the interface further.

Final evaluation

The System Usability Scale and the User Experience Questionnaire, both run against the old and the redesigned version, plus a heuristic evaluation by experts scoring the redesign against ten established usability principles.

The eight tested tasks, in the order an instructor meets them

Each of the nine participants performed all eight tasks under the Think Aloud protocol, on both the original and the redesigned platform.

Version 1 → Version 2

Four changes, and the reason each one was made.

Both versions live in the same Figma file and share the same design system, so the differences below are deliberate design decisions rather than drift. Use the Version 1 / Version 2 toggle, or the numbered pins, to move between them. Every screen opens at full size.

A. The action row: putting Save where it is found first

The user problem

Instructors lost work. Save sat fourth in a row of five controls at the far right of the editor, next to an unlabelled gear, where it read as one more option rather than the thing that protects your work.

Research evidence

The paper lists "the Save button is not prominent, leading to data loss" among the problems instructors reported, and a clear, prominent Save function among the pain points the analysis identified.

The design decision

Order the row by consequence, not by frequency: the one irreversible-if-missed action comes first, and every other control earns a text label. Instructors described the prominent Save button as contributing to a more positive experience.

Create New Assessment · both frames 1440 × 1121

B. The question editor: one commit action, honest defaults, real scoring rules

The user problem

In Version 1 the only filled button under a finished question said Preview, all four answer choices arrived already ticked as correct, and formatting had to be dug out of collapsed menus.

Research evidence

The paper records a text editor that is complex, especially for equations and special formatting — to the point that instructors drafted exams in Word first — alongside difficulty managing questions and a system lacking flexibility.

The design decision

Make writing a question and committing a question two visibly different acts, and stop pre-answering it for the instructor. Then add the scoring rules the editor had been silently deciding on their behalf.

Add new question · Multiple choice · matched 1440 × 1120 crop

Version 2 is 82 px taller than Version 1 because of the scoring block at its foot. It is trimmed to Version 1's height here so both sit at identical scale; the trimmed block is shown below, and "Enlarge" opens the untrimmed screen.

C. Preview, split in two: check the question, then check the exam

The user problem

"Preview" in Version 1 was a single word doing two jobs badly. It rendered the question you were editing, and it was the loudest button on the screen — but it never showed the assessment as a student would sit it.

Research evidence

The paper lists "there is no Student View for previewing exams" as a problem with the current platform, and inadequate preview among the identified pain points. Participants later highlighted the addition of the Student View as particularly valuable.

The design decision

Separate the two questions an instructor actually asks. Did this question come out right? stays inline as a quick preview. What will my class see? becomes its own named destination, reachable from the editor's action row.

D. After the exam: reading the results without reading them twice

The user problem

Version 1's results area asked instructors to accept whatever order the table arrived in, gave no sign of when scores were last calculated, and printed the same ranking twice — once as "hardest", once as "easiest".

Research evidence

Insufficient analytics and feedback features appear in both the problem statement and the pain-point analysis, and better analytics was one of the three key requirements drawn from the interviews. Improved analytics was later highlighted as particularly valuable.

The design decision

Let one table answer several questions by making it sortable and switchable, rather than duplicating it. Add the two things a table cannot say: how the cohort spread, and how fresh the numbers are.

Smaller corrections, carried through the whole file

Not every Version 2 change needed a research finding behind it. These came from reading Version 1 against its own rules — the kind of inconsistency the heuristic review scored most highly once it was gone.

The sidebar told you the wrong thing

On Version 1's Dashboard, the highlighted nav item was All Tests. Version 2 highlights Dashboard, and the breadcrumb becomes a real link.

One action, one name

The dashboard's recent-test cards said Submission while the All Tests table said View Submission for the identical destination. Version 2 settles on View Submission everywhere.

Support asks a better first question

Version 1's report form opened an empty textarea. Version 2 offers a list of common problems for the issue type you picked, so a report can be filed by recognition rather than composition. The submit button's spelling was fixed at the same time.

Version 2 walkthrough

One assessment, start to finish.

Every screen below is an export of a designed Version 2 frame. Steps the Figma file does not cover are not shown.

Step 1

Locate an assessment

Everything an instructor owns sits behind three tabs — Active, Draft and Archived — with search and a due-date sort above them. Clear categorisation of tests was one of the redesign's stated goals.

  • Status is a coloured pill, not a column of text
  • Each row carries View Submission, duplicate and delete
  • An empty tab says "No Test" rather than showing a blank table
All Tests — Active
All Tests — Archived, empty

Step 2

Create it, then configure it

The editor opens with Save leading the action row. Setting — the button that was a bare gear in Version 1 — holds everything about how the exam runs, including the group assignment and secure-exam controls the research asked for.

  • Dates, duration and attempts allowed
  • A generated password, and assignment to groups
  • Secure Exam Mode, off by default, with its effect spelled out
Create New Assessment

Step 3

Build the parts and questions

Parts become tabs across the top of Test Content, so a multi-part paper is one click wide rather than one long scroll. Inside a part, questions are draggable rows with their own points, a Show answer toggle, and edit and delete controls.

  • Multiple choice, fill in the blank, true/false, pair matching, essay, calculated formula and question sets
  • Points are set per question, inline
  • Each question type has its own designed editor and preview state

Add new question — Pair matching

Step 4

Or reuse what already exists

Copy from pool lists past assessments by term down the left, and expands the chosen one into its parts and questions on the right, each individually selectable. An instructor can take a whole paper, one part, or a single question.

  • Grouped by academic term
  • Checkboxes cascade from the paper, to the part, to the question
  • Points come across with each question

Step 5

See it as the class will

Student View renders the exam the way a student receives it: the countdown, the part selector, numbered question chips with a See All overview, and the student's own Report an Issue link in the corner — which is what feeds the Student Feedback & Issues tab further on.

Design adopted from Nichakan Poomipraphat's student-side redesign of the same platform, as recorded in the paper.

Student View

Step 6

Read the results

Submissions, Analytics and Student Feedback & Issues sit under one heading. The table handles individual marking and bulk actions; the analytics tab answers how the paper performed rather than how one student did.

  • Auto grade and upload, with a last-updated stamp
  • Force Submit for unfinished attempts, and a submissions download
  • Ranking Questions and Score Distribution side by side
View Submission — Submission
View Submission — Analytics

Step 7

Across every course: the dashboard

One level up from a single paper. Four summary tiles, two score charts across the term, and recent tests as cards that go straight to Edit or View Submission. Enhanced analytics and dashboard was one of the redesign's stated aims.

The figures shown are the design file's placeholder content.

Dashboard

Step 8

When something goes wrong

Contact Support asks for the issue type first — Question Creation, Exam Display, Grading & Calculation, Security, Account, Technical Issue, Suggestion or Other — then offers the problems commonly reported under it. Priority sets the expected response window in plain words rather than in labels only the team understands.

  • Low (1 week), Medium (1 day), High (immediately)
  • Screenshots and files can be attached

Measured, and mixed.

User Experience Questionnaire

Six dimensions, each rated from −3 to +3. Four rose, one fell and one did not move — so this is not an improvement across the board, and the paper does not present it as one.

What moved most

Dependability (+1.1) and Perspicuity (+0.6) are the two largest gains — whether the system feels predictable and controllable, and whether it is easy to understand. Those are the dimensions a visible Save, labelled controls and a consistent nav would be expected to move.

What did not

Stimulation fell from 0.3 to 0.1 and Novelty was unchanged at −0.25. The redesign made the platform clearer without making it more engaging or more original in participants' eyes. The paper reports no significance testing, so these figures describe the sample, not a proven effect.

Expert heuristic evaluation

The paper reports an average score of 7.3 out of 10 across ten usability principles. It gives no evaluator count and no baseline score for the old version, so this is a reading of the redesign on its own, not a comparison.

Reflection and next steps

Clearer, yes. Finished, no.

What became clearer

  • Where to save. Moving one button and removing its competition addressed the problem instructors described most concretely — losing work.
  • What a control does. Labelling the gear, renaming Preview to Student View and Report to Contact Support each cost nothing and removed a guess. Consistency scored 9/10 in the expert review.
  • What the system just did. A last-updated stamp and an unread count on the feedback tab let the interface answer questions instead of being asked them.
  • What the results mean. One sortable ranking and a score distribution replaced a table printed twice.

What still needs work

  • Error messages. The expert review scored error recovery 6/10: most messages are understandable, but some lack clarity or actionable guidance.
  • Flexibility for different workflows. Also 6/10. Some customisation is possible, but the system does not adapt to individual instructors' preferences — and instructors in this study did not all work the same way.
  • Visibility of system status. 6/10 again. The stamps and badges added in Version 2 are a start, not a finish.
  • Engagement. Stimulation fell and novelty did not move. Clarity was won; nothing was won on how the platform feels to use.

Next steps — proposed, not built

These follow from the limitations above and from the goals the paper lists for the redesign. None of them are implemented in the Figma file, and none are claimed as completed work.

  • Rewrite error messages so each one names the cause and the next action, then re-run the error-recovery heuristic.
  • Give the editor room for different working styles — the instructors who draft in Word are describing a workflow, not just a formatting problem.
  • Order question types by how often they are actually used, which the paper records as a mismatch in the current platform and which neither Figma version resolves.
  • Test the Scoring Setting, group assignment and Secure Exam Mode with instructors; they were added late and have no usability data of their own.
  • Establish whether the clarity gains hold over a term of real use, rather than a single session.
